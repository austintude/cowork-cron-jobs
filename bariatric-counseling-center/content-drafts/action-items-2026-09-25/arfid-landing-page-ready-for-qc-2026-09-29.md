# ARFID Treatment for Adults in Texas

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is an eating disorder in which a person eats too little, or too narrow a range of foods, because of sensory sensitivity, fear of choking or vomiting, or low interest in eating, not because of worries about weight or body shape (National Eating Disorders Association). It often begins in childhood, but it can continue into adulthood or start at any age (NEDA), and the Bariatric Counseling Center helps adults with ARFID in person in San Antonio and by telehealth anywhere in Texas.

Many adults with ARFID have spent years hearing that they are "just picky". If eating feels stressful, your list of safe foods keeps getting shorter, or meals with other people are something you plan your life around, it is worth talking to someone who takes it seriously.

## What ARFID looks like in adults

ARFID usually follows one or more of three patterns (NEDA):

- **Sensory.** Strong reactions to the taste, texture, smell or look of food, so only a few foods feel tolerable.
- **Fear-based.** Fear of choking, vomiting, pain or an allergic reaction, often after a frightening experience with food.
- **Low interest.** Little appetite or interest in eating; forgetting to eat; finishing meals feels like a chore.

In daily adult life that can mean eating the same few meals for years, relying on drinks or snacks instead of meals, avoiding work lunches, dates, travel or family gatherings, or feeling anxious for hours before a meal out. Under the diagnostic criteria, the eating pattern leads to weight loss or nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements, or real interference with daily life and relationships, and it is not explained by a lack of available food or a cultural practice (DSM-5, American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

## ARFID is not "just picky eating"

Plenty of adults dislike certain foods. ARFID is different in degree and in cost: the restriction affects health, nutrition, work or relationships, and it does not ease with willpower or a stern talk. Many people with ARFID have tried to "just eat it" many times. That usually makes the fear or disgust stronger, not weaker.

## ARFID is not anorexia

Both involve eating less than the body needs, but the reason is different. In anorexia the restriction is driven by fear of weight gain or distress about body shape. In ARFID it is not (NEDA). That difference changes treatment: ARFID care focuses on the fear, the sensory reactions or the low appetite, not on body image.

## ARFID can happen at any body size

ARFID is often pictured as a thin child. It is not limited to one body type. The diagnosis rests on nutritional, medical or everyday impact, not on a low weight (DSM-5), so adults in larger bodies can have ARFID and have it missed, sometimes for years, because a narrow diet built around a few safe foods does not look like "restriction" to anyone else.

Some people also develop a strong fear of eating after bariatric surgery, often after vomiting, pain or discomfort with certain foods. Always talk to your surgeon first, because medical causes need to be ruled out. When the fear itself is what keeps you from eating enough, behavioral treatment can help. ## How ARFID treatment works

NEDA names cognitive behavioral therapy for ARFID (CBT-AR) as one evidence-based approach, and describes ARFID care as a team effort that can include physicians, psychiatrists, therapists and dietitians (NEDA). In general, treatment works on a few things at once: understanding what drives the avoidance, learning skills for the anxiety or disgust that shows up around food, gradually and deliberately adding foods at your own pace, and rebuilding enough regular eating to cover what your body needs.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can adults have ARFID?
Yes. ARFID often begins in childhood, but it can continue into adulthood or begin at any age (NEDA). Many adults go years without a name for it.

### Is ARFID the same as picky eating?
No. Picky eating is a preference. ARFID is a pattern of avoidance or restriction that causes nutritional, medical or everyday harm, such as weight loss, relying on supplements, or avoiding work and social meals (DSM-5).

### How is ARFID different from anorexia?
In anorexia, restriction is driven by fear of weight gain or body image. In ARFID, it is driven by sensory sensitivity, fear of choking or vomiting, or low interest in food (NEDA).

### Does BCC treat ARFID in children or teenagers?
No. BCC serves adults only. For a child or teen, ask your pediatrician for a referral to a pediatric eating disorder program.

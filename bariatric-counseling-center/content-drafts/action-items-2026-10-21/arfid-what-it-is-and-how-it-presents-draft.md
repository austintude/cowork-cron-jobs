## What is ARFID?

ARFID stands for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder. It is an eating disorder in which a person eats too little, or too narrow a range of foods, to meet their nutritional or energy needs. Unlike anorexia, the restriction is not driven by a fear of weight gain or by body image. It is driven by the food itself: how it looks, smells or feels, a fear of what might happen when eating it, or simply very little interest in eating.

ARFID was added to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders in 2013. Many people think of it as a childhood problem, and it often starts in childhood, but it continues into adult life for a lot of people, and many adults have never had a name for what they live with.

## How ARFID presents in adults

ARFID tends to show up in one or more of three ways. A person can have more than one.

**Sensory sensitivity.** Strong reactions to the texture, smell, taste, temperature or appearance of food. The list of "safe" foods is short and very specific: one brand, one way of cooking it, nothing touching on the plate.

**Fear of what eating might cause.** Avoiding food after an experience of choking, vomiting, pain or severe nausea, or out of worry that it could happen. Some people stop eating whole categories of food, or only eat soft foods or liquids.

**Low interest in eating.** Forgetting to eat, feeling full quickly, or finding meals a chore rather than a pleasure.

In adult life, it often looks like this:

- Eating the same few meals every day and feeling real distress when they are not available.
- Skipping work lunches, team dinners or travel because of what the food might be.
- Dating, holidays and family meals feeling like something to get through.
- Relying on snacks, drinks or supplements to get through the day.
- Shame about eating "like a child", and years of being told to just try it.

## ARFID is not "just picky eating"

Plenty of people dislike certain foods. The difference with ARFID is the impact. The DSM-5-TR describes ARFID when the eating pattern leads to at least one of the following: significant weight loss or nutritional deficiency, dependence on supplements or nutritional drinks, or a clear effect on daily life, such as avoiding social situations because of food.

## ARFID is not anorexia

Both involve restriction, but the reason is different. In anorexia, restriction is tied to fear of weight gain and to how a person sees their body. In ARFID, it is tied to the food and the experience of eating. That difference matters because the treatment is different.

## ARFID can happen at any body size

ARFID is often pictured as someone who is underweight. In reality, it can happen at any body size. Someone who eats a narrow range of mostly processed, familiar foods can live in a larger body and still have ARFID, and that is one of the reasons it gets missed in adults.

## When ARFID and weight concerns overlap

This is where the Bariatric Counseling Center can help. Our Intensive Outpatient Program is built for adults who struggle with weight, binge eating and chronic overeating. When ARFID is part of the picture alongside weight concerns, it can be addressed within the same program:

- Group and individual therapy to understand the patterns behind eating.
- Registered dietitians who help widen the range of foods you eat and meet your nutrition needs, without a rigid meal plan.

- Our registered dietitian and registered nurse can coordinate with your medical providers.

## When a different level of care is the right fit

Some people with ARFID need a more specialized program. That includes people at a low body weight, people with very high anxiety or rigidity about trying new foods, and people who need significant accommodations to be able to eat. If that sounds like you, we will help you find a specialized eating disorder treatment program, and you can start that conversation with us.
